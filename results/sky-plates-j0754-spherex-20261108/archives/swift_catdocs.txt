  <p>The <acronym title='Second Swift XRT Point Source 
  Catalogue'>2SXPS</acronym> catalogue covers 3,790 square degrees on the sky and contains position, fluxes, spectral 
  details and variability information for 206,335 X-ray point sources
  detected in observations with the Swift X-ray Telescope between 2005 Jan 01 and 2018 August 01.
  Each source has a detection flag which indicates how likely 
  it is to be a real astrophysical object. <a class='int' href='#Table1'>Table 1</a> shows the false positive rate as a function of 
  flag and other summary information.</p>
  
  <p>Full details of how the catalogue was constructed are given in 
  <a href='paper.php'>Evans et al (2020)</a>. The underlying process
  was similar to <a href='/1SXPS'>1SXPS</a>, however with several key improvements.
  Most noteworthy of these were better modelling of pile up, improved background
  modelling, especially in the presence of stray light, and a refined
  <acronym title='point spread function'>PSF</acronym>. Additionally, larger stacked images
  were permitted than was possible in 1SXPS.</p>
  

  <p>On this web page we give some summary details of the catalogue; how to access, use and 
  cite it; a demonstration of its quality and performance; and finally details of 
  the catalogue contents. Other pages provide <a href='webdocs.php'>details about the 2SXPS website</a>
  and the <a href='uldocs.php'>upper-limit tool</a>.</p>
  
  <p>When <a href='webdocs.php#search'>Searching the catalogue</a> you can select a pre-defined subset
  of the full catalogue to search, for greater purity (but reduced completeness). There are two such samples, as defined in 
  <a class='int' href='#Table2'>Table 2</a>.</p>

  <h2 id='access'>Data Access and Usage</h2>
  
  <p>The catalogue can be queried <a href='index.php'>through the search interface</a>,
  or <a href='download.php'>downloaded as a FITS file, CSV file or SQL dump</a>.</p>
  
  <p>Not all columns contain values for each entry in the tables,
  for example, in the <a href='#sourceTab' class='int'><em>Sources</em> table</a>, only sources for which a spectral fit exists have entries in the
  <code>Fitted*</code> fields. In the CSV files, such empty entries are literally empty; in the FITS and SQL
  files these entries are <code>NULL</code>.</p>
  
  <p>  If you use data from this catalogue
  in your work, please cite <a href='paper.php'>Evans et al. (2020)</a> and in the
  acknowledgements section of your publication, please state:</p>
  <p style='margin: 10px; text-align:center; font-style:italic; font-weight: bold;'>This work made use of data supplied
  by the UK Swift Science Data Centre at the University of Leicester.</p>

  <!-- COMPLETENESS -->
  
  <h2 id='complete'>Completeness and purity</h2>
  
  <p>
    In order to evaluate the performance of the 2SXPS source detection system we made use
    of an extensive set of simulations. This is described in detail in Section 7 of 
    <a href='paper.php'>Evans et al. (2020)</a>. We simulated fields for a range of
    exposure times and background levels, chosen to reflect the range of such values
    in 2SXPS. Simulations did not include artifacts such as stray light,
    hot pixels, bright Earth or diffuse sources; however, our screening process
    is designed to identify and flag 2SXPS sources affected by such phenomena.
    We ran the 2SXPS source detection algorithm on the simulated images, and so
    by comparing the set of detected sources with the input list of simulated sources,
    we can measure both the completeness of our detection system and its purity.
  </p>
    
  <p>Each detection is assigned a flag based on various statistical tests.
  These flags were calibrated (via the simulations) such that the fraction of spurious
  sources was 0.3% (i.e. equivalent to a Gaussian 3-&sigma; significance) for <em>Good</em>,
  1% when <em>Good</em> and <em>Reasonable</em> sources are considered, and up to 10% when
  the <em>Poor</em> sources are also included. <a class='int' href='#figFAR'>Fig. 1</a>
  shows the false positive rate obtained from the simulations as a function of exposure
  time, validating the purity claims made here.</p>
  
  <div id='figFAR' class='figure' style='width: 400px; margin-top: 40px; float: left; margin-left: 80px'>
    <img  alt='False alarm rate as a function of exposure time and flag' src='figs/FAR.png' style='width: 100%;' />
    <p class='caption'><strong>Fig. 1</strong> False positive rate as a function of exposure
    time and flag.  The green points refer to <em>Good</em> sources, blue points are
    <em>Good</em> and <em>Reasonable</em> and red shows all sources.
    </p>
  </div>
  
  <div id='figComplete' class='figure' style='width: 400px; margin-top: 40px; float: right; margin-right: 80px'>
    <img  alt='False alarm rate as a function of exposure time and flag' src='figs/complete.png' style='width: 100%;' />
    <p class='caption'><strong>Fig. 2</strong> The completeness of 2SXPS. The dotted, solid and dash-dot lines show the
    flux at which 10%, 50% and 90% of the simulated sources were recovered, respectively. Colours are as in Fig. 1.
    </p>

  <!-- TERMINOLOGY -->
  
  <h2 id='terminology'>Notes on terminology</h2>
  
  <p>
    There are various phrases and labels used within the 2SXPS catalogue which it is important
    users properly understand, especially since the meanings of these labels may differ
    from those used in other catalogues. Several key pairs of labels are described here.</p>
  
  <h3>Datasets: observations and stacked images (and snapshots)</h3>
  
  <p>
    <em>Swift</em> data are organised into <em>observations</em>, each of which has a unique
    11-digit obsID. An observation generally contains all observations of a given location
    within a UT day, and may consist of multiple <em>snapshots</em>, taken on different spacecraft orbits, as described
    <a href='https://www.swift.ac.uk/support/obsidnew.php' title='Page describing how Swift observes'>on this page</a>.
    The basic datasets from which 2SXPS was created are the observations.
  </p>
  
  <p>
    Many locations on the sky are observed repeatedly by
    <em>Swift</em>, for example, to monitor the evolution of a specific
    source, and so we also  combined overlapping observations to give a
    single dataset of greater exposure, allowing fainter sources to be
    detected. We call these datasets <em>stacked images</em>. Some
    observations have only partial overlap, and one can make long
    &lsquo;chains&rsquo; of overlapping observations covering large
    spatial areas. We defined a maximum stacked image size of 2,300
    pixels (~90&prime;) to a side and then created stacked images such
    that every overlap of observations is in at least one stack. Thus,
    multiple stacked images can overlap, and an observation can
    contribute to multiple stacked images; but the maximum exposure at
    any given sky is reached for every location <em>Swift</em> has
    observed.
 </p>
 
 <p>Within each dataset (observation or stacked image), source detection
 and PSF fitting is carried out on each of the four
 bands <em>independently</em> of each other. Note that this is
 not the same approach as the XMM catalogues, in which the energy bands are handled
 simultaneously.</p>

  <p>
    There is a third natural timescale to consider for <em>Swift</em> data. The satellite is in a 
    low-Earth orbit and due to its Earth-limb avoidance constraints, the longest window of continuous visibility for
    any point on the sky is around 2.7 ks. Thus if an observation is required which is longer than this
    (or in which multiple exposures are desired within a UT day, for science reasons) the observation will comprise
    a number of separate exposures, known as &lsquo;snapshots&rsquo;. Each snapshot is a continuous exposure in a single pointing direction.
    We do not carry out source datection on individual 
    snapshots, however light curves are built with per-snapshot binning (as well as per-observation binning) and 
    variability calculations are also carried out on this timescale.    
  </p>
  
  <h3>Detections and sources; and aliases</h3>
  
  <p>
    In the creation of 2SXPS source detection was carried out on every dataset, and every energy band 
    independently (as noted above, this differs from the XMM approach). Thus for many sources there are multiple
    detections, in the different energy bands and in different datasets covering the source location. Details of these
    are all listed in the &lsquo;Detections&rsquo; table available for download, and are given in the relevant parts
    of the different web pages.
  </p>

  <p>
    These separate detections were then rationalised into a single list of unique source in two steps.
    First, detections across different bands but within the same dataset were combined. For this stage, only the 
    statistical errors on the source positions, as deduced from the PSF fit, were used to determine
    whether two detections corresponded to the same underlying source.  This is because, since all detections at this point are from the same
    dataset, the astrometric system is the same for all sources and so its uncertainty is irrelevant.
    Then the lists of unique soures per dataset were combined to create the final source list; at this stage the astrometric
    uncertainty associated with the datasets was taken into account.</p>

  <p>
    The process of merging detections was not perfect. Occasionally the statistical uncertainties were underestimated
    and also the astrometric uncertainty appears to have a broad tail. As a result of this a small number of sources
    exist for which multiple detections were not correctly merged, and instead the catalogue contains two distinct sources which really 
    are the same object. Based on an inspection of the distribution of source separations in the catalogue,
    we therefore identified any sources which lie within 20&Prime; of each other and are not detected in the same
    dataset, and labelled these as <em>possible</em> aliases of each other. It is vital to note that not everything so labelled
    is really an alias; in some cases they are genuinely distinct sources which are close together. In other cases, particularly where 
    one of the sources is very bright; the nearby sources are spurious detections in the PSF wings; this is not the same as an alias.
    In the case of genuine aliases, the available light curves of the sources will be incomplete and incorrect. That is, if a source is incorrectly identified as being two
    sources, called source A and source B, then the light curve of source A will not contain values
    derived from the blind detections that were assigned to source B, and furthermore the bins from those datasets where
    the source was called source B will have been deduced (for source A) based on the background map with the PSF model
    of source B present! Therefore, in the light curve section of the source pages, an option is given to consolidate
    across aliases of the source. Using this you can select which sources are genuine aliases, and the correct light curve will
    then be produced.</p>
  
  
  
  <h3 id='blindOrNot'>Blind and non-blind (retrospective) detections</h3>

  <p>
    The sources present in 2SXPS were detected via a blind search. That is, no a-priori knowledge
    of the possible existence of sources was used to deduce whether or not a given set of photons corresponded
    to the detection of a point source. However, once the list of sources had been produced, a retrospective
    check was carried out on those datasets in which the source was located, but had not been detected
    by the blind search. In this case the existence of a source at the specific location was assumed a-priori,
    and the count-rate of the source inferred based on the number of source and background events at the 
    source's location. If this count-rate was above zero, with 99.7% confidence (i.e 3-&sigma;), the source
    was deemed to have been present and thus detected in that dataset. <em>The detections database table only lists
    the blind detections</em>; sources identified as detected in this non-blind, retrospective analysis
    were not added to the table, however in the light curve analysis they are marked as being detected
    in those datasets. When viewing the light curves you have the option of whether non-detections
    should be shown as 3-&sigma; upper limits, or datapoints with 1-&sigma; error-bars, and you can specify this separately
    for bins in which the source was not detected in any way, and those in which there was no blind detetion, but
    in which the targeted analysis yields a non-zero count rate.
 </p>

  <p>The <em>Detections</em> table contains details of the specific detections that comprise the catalogue. Each 
  source in the <a class='int' href='#sourceTab'>Sources</a> table has at least one entry in this detections table; most sources have

  <!-- ISSUES -->
  
  <h2 id='issues'>Known issues and problems</h2>  
  
  <p>While every attempt was made to guarantee the accuracy of all of the data in this catalogue,
  some issues have since been identified, and where possible, mitigated. These are detailed below.</p>
  
  <dl title='bugs' id='bugList' class='columns'>
  
    <dt id='badArtFlag'>Incorrect flagging of some sources affected by diffuse emission.</dt>
      <dd>For fields in which areas of diffuse emission or artifacts were identified by human inspection and
      described by an elliptical region, the flagging of sources was incorrect. The elliptical region
      applied to identify affected sources was rotated by 90&deg; compared to the correct orientation.
      Thus, some detections in those fields have a <a class='int' href='#detFlagDef'>DetFlag</a> value
      with bit 4 incorrectly (un)set. <strong>Affected fields have bit
      5 (value 32) set in their <a class='int' href='#fieldFlagDef'>FieldFlag</a>. This issue has been fixed for future catalogues.</strong></dd>
    
    <dt>Poor pile-up correction in some light-curve bins.</dt>
     
     <dd>In a small number of cases, the pile up fitting to the soft, medium or hard energy bands is unreliable, to a
      degree that the count-rate in a specific observation and band is unreliable. This is simply a result of the stochastic
      nature of fitting pile up and determining whether the piled-up or non-piled up fit is better. There are 
      two problematic cases: when an object is found to be piled up in a sub-band but not the total band; or the pile-up correction
      factors in the total band and the sub-band differ by more than 50%. In these cases the light curve bin
      is flagged, and by default is excluded from the light curve displayed / downloaded; you have to specifically 
      request to include these bins.</dd>
    
    <dt>Source blending in crowded fields</dt>
      <dd>The process of identifying unique sources is a two-step process. The first step merges detections across the different
      bands <em>within the same dataset</em> into a list of unique objects in that dataset. In the second step, these &ldquo;obsSources&rdquo;
      are merged across all datasets into a unique source list. An error has been identified in the first step, whereby distinct by nearby
      detections in the same band can associated with the same object: being distinct detections in the same band they must, in fact,
      be distinct objects. This means that the number of sources in the catalogue is slightly underestimated.
      This issue has been fixed for future catalogues.</dd>
    
  </dl>
