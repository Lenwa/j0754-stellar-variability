J0830: DIRECT TESS PIXELS, COMPARISON STARS AND THE BIGGER QUESTION
11 November 2026 | Kyle Hicks and Ari

RESULT

A roughly five-week modulation survives direct extraction from calibrated
TESS images and correction against a bright comparison star. Its fitted
minimum-to-maximum amplitude is about 5%. The spatial attribution test is
partial: sector 46 is consistent with J0830's position; sectors 44 and 45
remain mixed with image-shape changes. The complete three-sector host
assignment remains unresolved.

This strengthens the evidence for a real signal in the target aperture,
without confirming a companion, exact synchronization or a fading mechanism.
The primary motion-corrected free peak is 37.39 days, compared with Gaia's
38.389-day candidate period. About two cycles are observed. These are nearby
timescales; the photometry does not supply a precision orbital-period test.

Fit a robust per-frame background plane after excluding all catalogue source
positions within 2.8 pixels. Extract fixed target radii of 1, 1.5 and 2 pixels,
and controls at 1.5 pixels. Apply QUALITY=0, finite-time/image and positive

control selection: 3,261/3,121/3,320 in S44/S45/S46, giving 348 matched 0.2-day
bins. No brightness clipping or outcome-based control removal is applied.

Fit periodic and nuisance terms jointly, with the existing 5–100-day frequency
grid. Compare sector offsets plus a global slope, individual sector slopes,
and separate arc offsets. For spatial maps, fit each pixel's signed sine/cosine
coefficients at the fixed Gaia candidate period, using the target aperture's
phase. A signed map avoids treating every noisy positive pixel as a source.

PERIOD AND COMPARISON RESULTS

For the unregistered 1.5-pixel aperture, the global-slope free peak is 38.59 d,
with 2.65% semiamplitude at Gaia's period. Correcting only against bright
control 1 gives 39.13 d and 2.50%. All three direct aperture sizes have free
peaks between 37.73 and 40.44 d across the three declared nuisance models.
Their fixed-Gaia semiamplitudes span 2.02–2.73%.

The two controls more strongly favor about 13 days, not the target's five-week
wave. Fainter control 2 has substantial excursions and yields unstable
corrections. It remains in all original ensemble and single-control results.
The two-control ensemble is not represented as a clean confirmation.

Unregistered spatial maps show shared positive/negative dipoles around bright
stars, exposing image motion. One bounded follow-up aligns frames using only
the bright comparison star's measured position. The target does not define
the alignment. Use a fixed 2.5-pixel centroid window, cubic translation and a
two-pixel crop for maps. Maximum measured absolute shifts are <=0.136 pixels.
This follow-up is recorded as post-initial analysis, not part of the initial
prediction. Interpolation changes undersampled peak pixels; no calibrated
positional error or interpolation-completeness claim is made.

After alignment, the sector-linear results are:

Extraction                       | Free peak | Gaia semiamplitude | Power
Primary target radius 1.5         | 37.39 d   | 2.48%              | 0.932
Primary / bright-control flux    | 37.39 d   | 2.49%              | 0.910
Target radius 2.0                | 37.90 d   | 2.50%              | 0.935
Radius 2.0 / bright-control flux | 37.90 d   | 2.52%              | 0.907

Power is fractional residual sum-of-squares improvement over the same
nuisance-only model, not confidence or a false-alarm probability. The small
registered radius 1.0 aperture fails this agreement: the sector-linear scan
instead peaks at about 60 days. Its failure remains visible, and prevents
a claim that every aperture/correction recovers the same period.

SPATIAL RESULT AND STOPPING POINT

For S46, the registered modulation center is about 0.23 pixels from the
reference source's fitted center, and 0.19 pixels from the propagated Gaia
position. Its morphology is consistent with light from the target vicinity.

For S44 and S45, the descriptive modulation-reference offsets are 1.13 and
0.89 pixels. Both modulation Gaussian fits hit their lower width bound,
while the maps retain mixed signs and image-shape structure. These fits do
not identify a displaced star and do not cleanly localize the signal. A
stationary-plus-motion template fit is also inconsistent across sectors
(-4.00%, +1.35%, +3.04%); it is not used as a stellar-amplitude measurement.

A G=17.331 Gaia neighbour lies about 1.9 TESS pixels from J0830. Catalogue
G magnitudes alone do not set a TESS variability/contamination limit. Fainter
or unresolved blends remain possible. The spatial test therefore supports
the target vicinity in S46 but leaves the full host assignment open.

The predeclared weakening outcomes mattered: aperture sensitivity, a poor
faint control and incomplete localization are retained. Stop after this one
motion-aware follow-up. Further tuning of the same two-cycle record would
yield less independent information than a separate epoch, ground photometry,
radial velocities or specialist review.

PULLING BACK ACROSS THE RECENT WORK

The main scientific question remains: what causes the long fading episodes?
The archive search, comparison-giant screen and sibling analysis add context;
they have not independently solved that question.

J0754: our strongest case. Historical and modern fading/recovery are replicated;
optical fading is larger than infrared fading. Roughly 36-day variability,
chromospheric evidence and X-rays support an active giant. Evolving spots
are a leading working explanation, with a Gaia companion candidate supplying
a possible way to maintain activity. Activity is not an orbit confirmation,
and the measured spot-wave amplitude does not directly measure total dark
surface coverage. The existing amplitude/brightness tests did not establish
a simple magnetic-cycle law for the long fade. Nonuniform obscuration remains
among the surviving alternatives.

J0830: a useful comparison candidate. QLP and direct-image reductions of one
2021 epoch recover a roughly five-week signal near its Gaia candidate
timescale. Its 2015 K2 epoch does not repeat the same dominant period, and
this source-attribution test remains partial. The published historical fading
has only screening-level re-examination, not J0754's differential replication.

J0736: a weaker comparison clue. Its strongest photometric periods are roughly
19–21 days, near half a 39.42-day Gaia candidate orbit, with an inconsistent
waveform. That does not establish rotation, ellipsoidal modulation or a
shared mechanism with the other two stars.

The useful synthesis is a testable active-giant/binary connection across the
historically selected stars. The decisive missing link is a measurement that
distinguishes surface-temperature/spot changes from intervening material as
the long fade evolves. Calibrated RV/activity spectra and simultaneous colors
would address that directly. We should now package the bounded archival
results for astronomer review rather than turn the same data into increasingly
precise-looking period claims. No prevalence or coincidence probability is
assigned to three retrospectively noticed Gaia candidate periods.
